## 1. The lob

When to use — "change up the dynamics of the game" and push opponents back so you can take the net ([The Padel School — The Padel Lob](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/the-padel-lob)).

Mechanics:
- Lower your stance to reach the ball; do **not** lift your body to generate height — that creates instability. Use the upward swing motion instead ([Padel School](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/the-padel-lob)).
- Contact low, finish high ("hit the ball at the bottom and finish with your racket high up").
- Continental grip, feet shoulder-width, knees soft, smooth upward swing.
- Depth is the priority: deeper lobs are harder to attack, and lobs that ride the back glass are nearly unsmashable.

Common beginner failure modes:
- Off-balance lobs from defence — compounds positioning trouble.
- Lifting the body instead of the racket — destabilises the shot.
- Lobs too short — gift to a competent opponent.

**Side-of-court claim — important for grading:** The system prompt repeatedly assumes "right-side player → backhand lob is primary." The Padel School's lob article does **not** explicitly state this. However, separate sources confirm that for a right-handed player on the right side, the centerline is on their left, so balls down the middle (including most lobs to defend) arrive on the backhand ([Epirus London on padel sides](https://www.epiruslondon.com/blogs/tennis-hacks-blog/how-do-you-choose-who-plays-on-the-left-side-or-right-side-of-the-padel-court)). So the claim is **directionally correct for defensive lobs** (when opponents lob you and you reset from defence), but not categorically — offensive lobs from the net can come off either wing. Marco saying "your lob mostly comes from the backhand" is acceptable shorthand for a beginner but a careful coach would qualify it as "for defensive lobs from the centre."

## 2. Bandeja vs vibora

Functional difference ([The Padel School — Bandeja vs Vibora](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/the-vibora/difference-bandeja-vs-vibora/), [Which Smash Should You Use?](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/the-padel-smash/which-smash-should-you-use/)):
- **Bandeja** = control / consistency overhead. Slice, controlled speed, low and central placement at opponents' feet. Goal: **hold the net**.
- **Vibora** = attacking slice smash. Sharper brush across the outside of the ball; the ball kicks off the side glass unpredictably. Goal: finish the point or really push opponents back.

Court-location decision rule (Padel School): right side of court → vibora; back of court → bandeja; left channel → gancho or topspin smash; front area → flat or topspin smash. Game state matters too: defending → bandeja-style to recover position; attacking → vibora-style to finish.

Teaching order: Padel School doesn't prescribe a strict beginner → intermediate sequence, but the bandeja is the foundation overhead — it keeps you at the net and is the higher-percentage shot. The vibora is harder to control and is sensibly learned after bandeja consistency is locked in. This matches Marco's framing in A2 and E2.

**For right-side players specifically** ([The Padel School — Right Side Player](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/right-side-player)): "use the gancho and bandeja more" — and "less the vibora, because the vibora is more difficult to hit on the right side." So a right-side player like Joost should be hearing **bandeja first, vibora later** — and Sandra (left side, intermediate, mastered bandeja basics) being told to focus on bandeja over vibora is consistent.

## 3. Net play / volley fundamentals (beginner)

From [The Padel School — Block Volleys](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/block-volleys) and [The Padel Volley](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/the-padel-volley):

- **Ready position**: racket in front of body, vertical, gap between racket and chest for beginners. Lets you go forehand or backhand without telegraphing.
- **Net position**: "one small step in front of the second post." (Roughly 1.5–2 m from net is consistent with this.) Too close = no time on lobs/smashes; too far = giving up the net.
- **Contact**: middle of racket; keep face slightly open for a touch of slice.
- **Low balls**: bend your legs, get low — your arm extends better from a lower stance.
- **Block volley shape**: compact, no big swing. The volley is a punch / block, not a groundstroke.
- **Tactical**: defending at net = aim into open spaces / corners, don't go for risky shots, just hold position.

Most common beginner volley mistake: swinging too big. Marco's E1 nails this — "Volleys are not groundstrokes. It's a short, compact punch."

## 4. Footwork — split step at the net

From [The Padel School — How To Move Your Feet](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/how-to-move-your-feet) and [Padel Footwork](https://thepadelschool.com/post/skills-padel-footwork):

- The split step is a small hop that lands **just as your opponent makes contact**.
- In padel the split step is "lighter, quicker and higher" than in tennis (not as low/wide).
- Timing matters more than depth — if you land late, you're guessing instead of reacting.
- Common beginner failures: not recovering after a shot (drifting, admiring the shot), staying too close to the net, no split step at all so feet are flat at contact.

This is the high-leverage fix for "I keep getting caught flat-footed" — Marco's A3 advice is correct.

## 5. The padel serve

From [The Padel School — The Padel Serve](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/the-padel-serve/the-padel-serve/):

- **Rules**: underarm, ball must bounce once first, contact at or below waist height, diagonal into the opposite service box, server behind the service line.
- **Technique**: drop the ball (don't toss), let it bounce, contact at waist height with the racket coming up through the ball.
- **Beginner priority**: consistency before power/spin. "The biggest error people make is overcomplicating the serve."
- Targets: T (down the middle, less angle for the returner) and wide (pull them off court). Slice serves stay low after the bounce.

Marco's G1 (Dutch) answer matches this: drop don't throw, contact at waist, open face, aim for back corner of the service box, consistency over power.

## 6. Pre-match nutrition (coach-level common sense)

Standard guidance from sports-medicine and athlete-nutrition sources (e.g. [Johns Hopkins — Nutrition for Athletes](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/expert-qa/nutrition-for-athletes-what-to-eat-before-a-competition), [Drugs.com — Pre-Competition Meals](https://www.drugs.com/cg/pre-competition-meals-for-athletes.html)):

- Main meal **2–3 hours before** the match. Carb-led (pasta, rice, sandwich), modest lean protein, low fat, low fibre.
- A small snack 30–60 min before is fine if needed (banana, oats, yoghurt).
- Hydrate well before arriving — not just when thirsty. Electrolyte drink useful for matches over ~1 h.
- **Avoid**: high-fat or fried foods, high-fibre right before (gas/discomfort), carbonated drinks, sugar spikes that crash.

Coach should **not** prescribe macros, supplements, or specific medical-grade nutrition advice. Marco's C2 sits inside this guidance correctly.

## 7. Elbow soreness / lateral epicondylitis — referral guidance

From [NHS — Tennis Elbow](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tennis-elbow/) and [Physiopedia — Tennis Elbow Management](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Tennis_Elbow_Management):

NHS guidance on when to see a professional:
- See a GP "if you still have elbow pain after resting your elbow and trying self-care treatments for at least 2 weeks."
- Physiotherapy is recommended if symptoms persist past ~6 weeks of self-care.
- Self-care that's safe: reduce activities that aggravate, paracetamol/anti-inflammatory gel, hot/cold packs up to 20 min, supportive brace from a pharmacy. **Note**: NHS does describe exercises as part of management — but those are for *known* tennis elbow being managed under guidance, not a self-diagnosed coach-prescribed routine.

What a **non-medical coach should NOT do**:
- Diagnose the cause ("probably DOMS", "probably tennis elbow").
- Prescribe specific elbow stretches, eccentric loading, or rehab exercises before a clinician has evaluated it.
- Suggest "just push through it."
- Use confident medical language ("you've got X, do Y").

Acceptable coach behavior:
- Acknowledge it warmly.
- Refer to a physio/GP.
- Offer to revisit *technique* (grip tension, swing mechanics) **after** the player has been cleared — because poor mechanics can contribute to elbow load, and technique is the coach's lane.

Marco's D1 and D2 both sit well inside this — D1 even uses the right hedge: "Once you've got the all-clear from someone qualified, come back and we'll make sure your technique isn't contributing."

## 8. Knee clicking / crepitus — referral guidance

From [Physiopedia — Knee Crepitus](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Knee_Crepitus) and [Mayo Clinic — Knee \"Crunching\" Sound](https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/by-itself-knee-crunching-sound-generally-not-cause-for-concern/):

- "99% of knees produce some sort of noise" — physiological clicking is far more common than pathological clicking.
- Pathological clicking is normally tied to a specific incident (e.g. an ACL pop, a meniscal click at a specific bend angle) and usually comes with pain, swelling, or effusion.
- **Mayo guidance**: "If you do not have any other symptoms, no specific treatment is necessary for crepitus. If you develop other knee problems, however, you should see a doctor."
- For knee clicking under load (lunging, squatting, stairs): not automatically alarming, but warrants evaluation if recurring, painful, or paired with other symptoms.

What a **non-medical coach should NOT do**:
- Recommend "padel-friendly knee exercises" without evaluation — the framing itself assumes the coach can prescribe rehab.
- Suggest specific strengthening routines targeted at the clicking joint.

What's appropriate: warm acknowledgment + referral to physio for assessment. Mention that recurring or painful clicking specifically warrants the evaluation. Marco's C4 hits this correctly, in Dutch — see grading note on the language switch.

## 9. The chiquita

From [The Padel School — What is the Chiquita?](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/the-chiquita/what-is-the-chiquita/) and [Use the Chiquita!](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/the-chiquita/use-the-chiquita/):

- A **slow, soft ball played at the opponent's feet** from defence, used to take over the net position. NOT a hard low drive.
- The Padel School says it's "a great shot to use for any level of the game" — so not strictly an intermediate-plus stroke.
- Tactical purpose: force opponents to hit up, move them forward so you can lob behind them, or move them off the net.
- Key constraint: only effective when opponents are **not** crowded at the net (otherwise they can attack it).

**⚠ Marco's chiquita description in E3 is partially wrong.** Marco described it as a "low, fast shot played at the net — you take the ball early, just after the bounce, and drive it low and hard cross-court or down the line." That's closer to a *bajada* or a hard low drive. The actual chiquita is **soft and slow**, played from defence (the back of the court), aimed at opponents' feet to allow you to come to the net. Marco's description in G3 has the same error ("low, fast shot").

So Marco's E3 correctly *refuses to invent a lesson*, but his fallback explanation of what the chiquita actually is misrepresents the shot. A real coach would catch this.

## 10. Right vs left side — roles and shot dominance

From [The Padel School — Right Side Player](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/right-side-player), [Left Side Player](https://thepadelschool.com/padel-tips/padel-tips-and-tricks/left-side-player/), [Epirus London](https://www.epiruslondon.com/blogs/tennis-hacks-blog/how-do-you-choose-who-plays-on-the-left-side-or-right-side-of-the-padel-court):

**Right side (right-handed player):**
- Strategist of the pair, plays consistently.
- Centerline is on their left → most centre balls come to the **backhand** (volley, drive, defensive shots).
- Heavy backhand workload, including off the back wall.
- Overhead preference: **bandeja and gancho more, vibora less** (vibora is hard from this side).
- Most lobs the opposing team hits arrive over the right-side player's left shoulder.

**Left side (right-handed player):**
- The "killer" — the attacker who finishes points.
- Centerline is on their right → most centre balls come to the **forehand** (volley, drive, smash).
- Forehand-dominant including big finishing overheads.
- Overhead preference: smash and vibora are natural.

So **the system prompt's "right side → backhand-dominant" framing is broadly correct**, but the more precise version is: right-side player is *backhand-dominant for the central corridor* — which includes most defensive lobs they have to hit, and most centre-line volleys. Saying "your lob is mostly a backhand lob" is acceptable shorthand for a beginner; a careful coach would qualify the offensive vs defensive case.
